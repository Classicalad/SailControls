    The demonstration

    Set your trim for a nice 10-knot beat, then press Let the breeze build and touch nothing. The wind goes to 22 while every string stays exactly where you left it. Watch the draft slide aft and the boat fall over — that is what you are fighting when you pull tension on.

    Five things a sail can be

    everything below moves one of these

    Every string on the boat is a way of changing one of five numbers. Learn the five and the controls stop being a list to memorise — they become the tools you reach for once you have decided what the sail should look like.

    DEPTH

    How fat it is

    Camber, as a percentage of the chord. Deep sail = big lift coefficient = power, and a lot of side force with it. Flat sail = less drive, but less heel and a wider steering groove. In the model this is the number that decides whether you are powered or pinned.

    DRAFT POSITION

    Where the fat bit is

    How far aft of the luff the deepest point sits, around 45–50% for a mainsail. Forward of that the entry is fine and the exit flat — fast and forgiving. Aft of it the leech hooks: the back of the sail is now a brake pointed sideways, and the helm loads up.

    TWIST

    How much the top gives away

    The angle between the top of the sail and the bottom. Some is required — the wind up there genuinely comes from a different angle. The rest is a choice about how much load you want up high, where it hurts most.

    ANGLE OF ATTACK

    How hard it faces the wind

    Sheet the sail in and you increase it: more lift until the flow separates, then all drag. Ease it and the luff starts to lift. This is the fast-twitch control — it changes gust to gust, not day to day.

    ENTRY

    How blunt the front is

    A round entry is tolerant — it takes a wide range of angles without complaining, which is what you want in waves. A fine entry points higher but demands accurate steering. On the jib, forestay sag is what sets it.

    THE RELATIONSHIP

    Nothing moves alone

    Pull the vang and you change twist and depth and draft position, because bending the mast takes luff round out of the front of the sail. That coupling is why trim feels hard, and why the model above shows you all five at once.

    Why more breeze means more tension

    three separate reasons, often confused
    REASON ONE

    You are only holding the shape you already had

    Aerodynamic pressure goes with the square of wind speed. Going from 10 to 20 knots is not twice the load on your rig — it is four times. Cloth stretches, the leech stretches, the forestay sags, the mast bends further. Leave every string exactly where it was and the sail becomes deeper, with the draft further aft and a leech that has bladed off at the top. So the first tranche of tension is not depowering at all. It is standing still.

    REASON TWO

    Past a point, power stops being speed

    Two people on the wire can hold a fixed amount of heeling moment. Below that number, more power is more speed. Above it, extra power just heels the boat, which spills it anyway — but now with the foils at a bad angle, the rudder dragging and the bow tripping. Once you are at the limit, the fast setting and the survivable setting are the same setting: flat, twisted, draft forward. Flattening is not slowing down. It is converting side force you cannot use into a boat you can steer.

    REASON THREE

    Draft aft is a handling problem, not just a slow one

    A hooked leech pushes the centre of effort aft and up. The boat develops weather helm, wants to round up, and every gust becomes a fight rather than an acceleration. Cunningham on, outhaul on, vang on: the draft comes forward, the exit opens, the effort moves down and forward, and the helm goes light. In a fully powered breeze the boat that is easiest to sail is the one that is set up flat — and the one that is easy to sail is the one that goes fast for the whole beat, not for ten seconds.

    The other direction is just as real

    Everything above runs backwards in light air, and this is where the tension habit costs people races. In 5 knots the cloth is not loaded, the forestay is not sagging and nothing needs holding in place. Cunningham on pulls the draft too far forward and kills the entry; outhaul on takes away the only depth you have; vang on closes a leech that has almost no flow energy to spare and the sail stalls. Light air wants everything soft and a little more twist than feels right. The controls are not a ratchet that only goes one way.

    Why the top of the sail is not the bottom

    Wind is slowed by friction against the water, so the true wind at the head of your rig is stronger than the true wind at the boom. Your boat speed, though, is the same at every height. Apparent wind is true wind minus boat velocity — so where the true wind is weak, down low, the apparent wind gets dragged further forward; where it is strong, up high, it stays further aft. The top of the sail is being hit from a different angle than the bottom, and twist is how the sail matches it.

    That shear is worth a few degrees — the model above shows you the live number. Real twist is bigger than that, because the second job of twist is deciding where to put the load. Load high on the rig has the longest lever on heeling moment, so twisting the head open is the cheapest power you can throw away. And here is the skiff twist: a 14 going quickly makes so much of its own apparent wind that the shear angle collapses. Once you are up and firing, twist stops being about matching the wind and becomes purely a depowering choice — which is why powered-up, in control, a fast 14 upwind carries a surprisingly tight leech.

    The two ladders

    in order, top first
    Overpowered — climb down
    1. Crew weight out and back. Free, instant, and it costs nothing in sail shape. Always first.
    2. Ease the sheet, play it. Angle of attack is the fastest lever you own. In a puff, dump and bear away a touch.
    3. Vang on. Bends the mast, flattens the bottom, opens the head — and now the sheet can be eased without the leech closing up. This is vang sheeting, and it is the technique that makes a traveller-less skiff work.
    4. Cunningham on. Drags the draft back forward from where the load pushed it. Cheap, big, reversible.
    5. Outhaul on. Bottom third goes flat.
    6. Ease the lowers. Let the middle of the mast bow forward; it pulls luff round out of the sail and the bottom half goes flat. Not so far that it can invert.
    7. Caps on. Takes the sag out of the forestay so the jib stops being a power sail — and adds bend while it is at it.
    8. Forestay out, rake aft. The whole-rig answer when the above has run out. Take some board with it or you will fight the helm, and expect to want more cap tension afterwards.
    Underpowered — climb up
    1. Everything soft. Cunningham fully off, outhaul off, vang just snug enough to stop the boom skying.
    2. Caps off, forestay short, lowers on. The rig stands up, the mast stays straight, the bottom half of the main gets deep, and the centre of effort sits high where the wind actually is.
    3. Sheet with twist. Do not haul the leech shut chasing height — low-energy flow separates easily and a stalled sail makes no lift at all.
    4. Jib lead forward and in to keep a closed but not choked leech, and let a little forestay sag give the jib a round, forgiving entry.
    5. Heel to leeward a few degrees. Gravity sets the sail, reduces wetted surface, gives the foils some angle. It is a trim control in drifting conditions.
    6. Move as little as possible. Every lurch dumps the flow off both sails.

    Flying the kite

    one rule, then three refinements

    A mainsail is trimmed to a shape. A spinnaker is trimmed to an edge. Everything else about flying it is detail hung off that one idea.

    THE RULE

    Ease until it curls, then stop

    Let the sheet out until the luff just folds in on itself, then take back the smallest amount that makes the fold disappear. That is it. Sitting on that edge, the kite is making every bit of drive it has in it. A hand's width further out and the luff collapses and it is pulling nothing — a soft kite is not a gentle setting, it is no setting. A hand's width in and it is still full, still looks lovely, and has quietly stopped being a wing. The curl is the only instrument you have, so keep the sheet moving and keep finding it.

    TACK LINE

    High when deep, down when reaching

    Eased, the tack floats up off the sprit and the whole sail lifts and rotates to windward — more area projected where the wind actually is, which is what running deep is about. Pulled down, the luff goes straight and tight and the sail holds its shape at the sharper angles a reach demands. The model will show you the camber moving as you slide it: roughly 23% deep with the tack flying, 18% with it strapped down. And in a lot of breeze, a high tack is also precisely what puts the load high and forward — which is to say, what buries the bow.

    ANGLES

    You gybe through them

    Watch the apparent wind readout as you bear away: sailing deep in a 14, boat speed eats the true wind and the apparent can drop to a fraction of it. That is why you do not sail dead downwind. You gybe through angles, trading distance for pressure, and the kite tells you when you have gone too deep by going soft and refusing to sit on a curl. As for the halyard — it goes to the top and stays there. Easing it to let the sail fly clear of the rig is a symmetric-kite trick from boats that genuinely do run dead square; an asymmetric tensions its luff between head and tack, so a low hoist just lets the luff sag.

    The limit downwind is not heel

    Upwind, what stops you is the boat falling over. Downwind, it is the bow going under. A big kite with the tack up, loaded in a breeze, puts an enormous force high and a long way forward of the mast, and the bow is what pays for it. That is why the tack line comes down as the wind builds, why the crew goes aft, and why nobody cleats the kite sheet. Past a certain point the honest answer is not a trim setting at all — it is that the kite comes down.

    What is particular about a Fourteen

    NO TRAVELLER

    The sheet does two jobs

    With the mainsheet coming to a bridle or a centre point rather than a car, you cannot change boom angle and leech tension separately. So you set the leech with the vang and then use the sheet purely for angle. Get the vang right and the boat becomes enormously easier to sail in a breeze.

    SQUARE TOP

    The head depowers itself

    A big roached head on a bendy tip is a gust valve: pressure loads the head, the tip twists off, the sail sheds it, the tip springs back. It buys you a lot of automatic gust response — and it means the vang has real authority over how much of that valve is available.

    APPARENT WIND

    You make your own weather

    A 14 reaching can put the apparent wind well forward of the beam in a true wind that is nearly behind. That is why the kite sheet and the main are trimmed almost like upwind sails at speed, and why the boat accelerates into its own wind rather than being pushed along by the true one.

    FOILS

    Rake changes the balance too

    Rig rake, board rake and rudder foil all pull on the same balance. Raking the rig aft to depower moves the centre of effort aft; if the underwater centre does not move with it, the beat is sailed with the rudder cocked and you have added drag exactly when you can least afford it.

    TWO ON THE WIRE

    The limit is a fixed number

    The righting moment available is roughly constant once both of you are out. That makes the overpowered/underpowered threshold sharp and predictable — and it is why the transition from "sheet it on" to "flatten everything" happens over a surprisingly narrow band of wind.

    DEVELOPMENT CLASS

    Your boat is not this boat

    Fourteens differ enormously — rig, spreaders, mast section, sail cut, control systems. Take the directions from this model and the numbers from your own tuning chart and your own boat, in your own conditions, with a stopwatch and someone to line up against.

    A teaching model, not a velocity prediction program. The aerodynamics here are simplified relationships chosen to make the couplings between controls visible and correct in direction — deeper sails make more power and more heel, load moves draft aft, sag rounds the jib entry, tension holds shape. Absolute numbers are illustrative. Anything you are about to bolt to a boat should be checked against your class tuning guide and your sailmaker.
